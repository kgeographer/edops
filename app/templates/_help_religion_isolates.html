Each society is ranked three separate ways against the other societies holding this same trait value. Ancestral — how rare the trait is within the society's own language family; 1/28 means one of 28 corpus members holds it. Geographic — distance to the nearest society of an unrelated family with the same trait. Environmental — distance in five-dimensional environmental space to the nearest society with the same trait. Overall sorts by each society's worst rank across the three, so a society high on all three rises to the top.

The rankings are not combined into a score, and there is no threshold: nothing here is flagged as an isolate or not. The order is the whole output, and where the three measures disagree is often more informative than where they agree.

What a high rank does not establish. All three measures are defined relative to other holders of the trait. For a practice that spread from a place of origin, the outer edge of that spread will be far from the bulk of holders, unrelated to them, and in a different environment — the same signature as an independent occurrence. This view cannot distinguish the frontier of cultural diffusion from convergent invention, and for transmissible traits the former is the more likely reading. The Ethnographic Atlas variables used here carry no coding for religious contact or missionization, so that check is not available.

Other limits. Some societies rank as remote because their region is thinly sampled in D-PLACE rather than because they are truly isolated. Environmental distance measures atypicality within this trait group, not against the world's environments — a society can top this ranking while sitting somewhere globally ordinary. Societies without a basin assignment are excluded from the environmental ranking; because those are mostly islands and coasts, some of the strongest candidates for genuine environmental atypicality are missing from it. Nothing here is a significance test.
